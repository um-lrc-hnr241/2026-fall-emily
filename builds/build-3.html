Build 3

Hegel, Fanon, and Reflexivity

Zoe Horwitz Honors 241, Build 3

In my first build, I understood Fanon’s search for “resistance, opposition, challenge” as an external circumstance that gives someone a chance to act for themself. However, rereading Hegel changed my understanding of why this opposition matters. Hegel does not suggest that difficult circumstances alone produce self consciousness; he argues that it develops through another self-consciousness, as each person becomes aware of himself in the encounter with the other. This connects to reflexivity, the examination of awareness, which often arises in moments of crisis that demand more than an impulse response. Through Hegel, Fanon’s search for opposition can therefore be understood less as a search for circumstances that produce independence and more as a search for a relationship that forces consciousness to turn back upon and recognize itself.

Hegel first establishes the importance of another consciousness when he writes “self- consciousness exists in and for itself by way of its existing so for another; that is, it exists only by being recognized” (Hegel 2). Another consciousness does not simply recognize an independence that already exists. The encounter shapes how a person becomes conscious of himself in the first place. Each consciousness sees itself in the other, which makes self- consciousness reflexive rather than merely internal. For this reason, opposition matters in Fanon, and not because consciousness turns external resistance into independent action. The encounter itself creates a situation in which consciousness must confront its own existence in relation to another consciousness. Hegel paints this picture of how reflexivity develops through his servant example. After experiencing the fear of death, the servant’s entire sense of stability shatters, as “everything solid and stable has been shaken to its foundations” (Hegel 5). Yet, Hegel explains that although “the fear of the master is the beginning of wisdom,” it is through work that “the servant becomes conscious of what he truly is” (Hegel 5). Through labor, the servant can recognize his own activity in what he produces, and Hegel even calls this “a rediscovery of himself by himself,” through which the servant “acquires a mind of his own” (Hegel 5). Fear creates the crisis that begins this reflexive process, but labor and discipline allow the servant to develop a different awareness of himself. Even though his external position remains dependent, his consciousness begins to change.

Fanon complicates this process because the historical relationship he describes does not follow the same development. Fanon explains that the formerly enslaved French Black person “did not fight for his freedom” because emancipation was granted by the white master (Fanon 9). He emphasizes that “the upheaval reached the Negroes from without” and that “the black man was acted upon” (Fanon 9). In my first build, I interpreted this primarily as evidence that granted freedom could not produce independence in the same way as freedom actively pursued. Hegel suggests a more specific problem involving reflexivity: the external circumstance has changed, as the formerly enslaved person has become legally free, but the relationship between the two consciousnesses has not undergone the confrontation through which consciousness becomes aware of itself through another.

This distinction helps clarify Fanon’s description of the French Black man searching for “resistance, opposition, challenge” (Fanon 10). Resistance does not necessarily make someone independent. Instead, opposition provides an encounter between consciousnesses through which the self may become explicit to itself. This also changes my previous interpretation of Fanon’s distinction between “reactional” and “actional” behavior. I originally assumed that becoming actional meant transforming an external challenge into conscious action. However, Fanon does not necessarily equate action with consciousness. Hegel instead suggests that before independent action can even become a question, consciousness must become reflexive through its relationships and activity. Rather than asking how consciousness allows someone to overcome external influences, Fanon and Hegel pose another question: how does encountering another consciousness contribute to someone becoming aware of himself as a consciousness at all?
