Build 1

Fear, Labor, and the Emergence of Self-Consciousness

Chosen passage:

“(§193:) In these terms, the truth of the independent consciousness lies in fact within the servile consciousness. At first, this consciousness admittedly appears outside itself and not as the truth of self-consciousness. But just as mastery showed that its essential nature proves the reverse of what it wants to be, so too servitude will ultimately turn into the opposite of what it immediately is; as a consciousness forced back into itself, it will withdraw into itself and transform into a truly independent consciousness. (§194:) […] For this servile consciousness has been fearful, not of this or that particular thing, or merely at odd moments, but its whole being has been seized with dread. For it has experienced the fear of death—the absolute Master. In that experience it has been quite undone, has trembled in every fiber of its being, and everything solid and stable has been shaken to its foundations. Yet, this pure universal movement—the absolute melting away of everything stable—proves to be the simple, essential nature of self-consciousness itself, absolute negativity, pure being-for-self […] Furthermore, the servant’s consciousness does not dissolve everything stable merely in principle; in his service he actually brings this about. Through his service he rids himself of his attachment to natural existence in every single detail and gets rid of it by working on it.”

Raw matter bathes in its own indifference to humans until acted upon. Take solitary particles of rock, for example, eroding to the aimless calls of the wind, finding their ways to different crevices of the earth. Yet, we can transform those very rocks into what we intend: perhaps packed tightly into architectural slabs or into clay. While the rock undergoes obvious physical alterations, is it possible that something might also alter within a human being in this process of manipulation? That is, by imprinting one’s desires onto the physical world, might a human being come to discover something about the nature of their own consciousness?

In the Phenomenologies of Spirit, Friedrich Hegel suggests a relationship between self-consciousness and interaction with the external world via a master-servant dynamic between one figure who commands, and another who submits to the wishes of the other, performing laborious actions.The Servant becomes akin to an “object” (Hegel 4), a catalyst to reach the final products that the Master can indulge in. At the same time, we must recognize that only the Servant performs the actual act of connecting with the physical world through labor due to the Master’s restraints. Contrarily, the Master now experiences a disconnect between raw, physical materials, and their final product. A paradoxical conclusion is thus reached: the Master, by subjugating the Servant, has now become dependent on the Servant’s labor to reconcile any kind of relationship with physical reality. If the process of transforming the natural world can also transform the person performing it as Hegel implies, then the restraints placed on the Servant by the Master become a critical detail distinguishing the two beings.

Particularly, because the Servant is forbidden from externally acting on their own desires, Hegel suggests that their servitude actually turns inwards and becomes a “consciousness forced back into itself” (5). Paradoxically, through forced labor, the Servant demonstrates an internal ability to delay gratification of a final product, to keep his “desire held in check” to do anything outside of the Master’s wishes (5). This suggests that self-consciousness may not be revealed merely by the freedom to materialize one’s desires, but more critically the ability to cultivate an internal scrutiny of one’s own desires that comes from interacting with the physical world. More specifically, labor may cultivate a discipline that can both withhold immediate desires and allow us to become conscious of our inner desires as something distinct from our outer, immediate actions.

​ Still, even this new idea begs a further look into where such discipline originates from to begin with. Consistent with Hegel, the Servant’s discipline begins with nothing more than dread: a fear of the force of death itself which Hegel calls “the absolute Master” (5), so complete that the Servant had “trembled in every fiber of its being” (5). In essence, the capability to delay gratification of one’s immediate desires seems to arise in the presence of both outward restraints and greater existential fear. Yet, this complicates the internal and the external: the origins of such discipline are now difficult to separate from the greater forces imposing it. Hegel’s analysis of labor and servitude, then, suggests that self-consciousness cannot exist as solely an isolated, internal thought; it thrives in active relation with and resistance from physical, natural existence and other beings. However, this lacks clear resolution as to if such a self-consciousness is truly already in existence and simply given light through worldly interactions.

In this vein, fear, discipline, and formative activity appear as critical aspects to consider in the emergence of self-consciousness. The difficulty in Hegel’s account, however, is that none of these forces are evidently sufficient on its own to be deemed the origin of an inner transformation. Without labor, our fear remains inward and suppressed, yet without that initial absolute fear, work and discipline risk becoming an “empty self-centered attitude of accomplishment” rather than resistant, formative forces (6). Thus, this mutual dependence leaves unclear whether the process of achieving discipline through labor aids an existing self-consciousness in awakening, or whether perhaps consciousness initially formulates through causal forces, like fear, that compel its development.
