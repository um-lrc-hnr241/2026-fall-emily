Build 1

Recognition, Otherness, and Free Will

My central theme: If consciousness relies on recognizing otherness through social interaction, is consciousness really an autonomous agent that grants us free will?

Passage: Hegel 195: “Although the fear of the master is the beginning of wisdom, consciousness is not therein aware of being-for-self. Through work, however, the servant becomes conscious of what he truly is. [...The master’s desire has reserved to itself the pure negating of the object and thereby an unalloyed feeling of self. But that is the reason why this satisfaction is itself only a fleeting one, for it lacks objectivity and permanence. Work, on the other hand, requires desire held in check, fleetingness staved off; in other words, work forms and shapes the thing. [...]... It is in this way, therefore, that consciousness, as a worker, comes to see in the independent being [of the object] its own independence.”

Humans generally assume that we formulate our own consciousness and choices; yet Hegel’s master-slave dialectic argues that our formation of true self-consciousness relies on conflict and interaction with other consciousness. For example, suppose an individual was born in an abyss, isolated. This individual would be aware through touch with the outside world, or inner signals such as hunger or pain. However, without a point of comparison, can an individual be self-aware, or ever self-conscious? This creates a bidirectionally ambiguous situation: Does consciousness grant heightened awareness of our self-identity and the capacity to make decisions? Or does society, and one’s identity within it, create the sense of consciousness? One may believe they have complete autonomy over their consciousness, but can consciousness ever be autonomous if it's formulated by perceiving otherness in a society?

In Hegel’s master-slave dialectic, he describes how consciousness “exists only by being recognized” (178). In humanity’s social world, when an individual meets another individual, they “recognize themselves as being mutually recognized,” resulting from “essentially the return from otherness” (167). Through this interaction, the master-slave dialectic emerges. The two consciousnesses will struggle for recognition. However, for self-consciousness to actually manifest, both individuals cannot succeed. Naturally, one consciousness will assume the position of master, and the other becomes the servant. This interaction occurs because Hegel states that recognition begins unequally; no internalization or improvement of self occurs when both consciousnesses are content.

But does that mean that self-consciousness is discredited because it cannot originate without recognizing otherness? In the master-slave dialectic, it seems this social interaction actually fuels an individual to possess free will. In the passage, Hegel describes, “fear of the master is the beginning of wisdom, consciousness is not therein aware of being-for-self. Through work, however, the servant becomes conscious of what he truly is.” This reveals that besides the initial point of social comparison, a second phase of interacting with the world through labor is necessary in creating self-consciousness. This is further supported by the position of the master, in which Hegel describes, “The master’s desire has reserved to itself the pure negating of the object and thereby an unalloyed feeling of self.” So, the master never possesses the same desire that the slave possesses because he is so focused on suppressing the recognition of the other. There is no room to formulate his self-identity if he is oppressing others rather than learning about how others differ; in turn, he will not be able to discover what values, beliefs, or motivations define him. Hence, the slave will eventually discover his self-consciousness through labor that reveals to him his skills and goals. The slave learning about these two concepts grants him self-identity, which, again, the master will never achieve because he is only focused on the suppression of another consciousness.

Although the formulation of self-consciousness begins through the relationship between two individuals, the master-slave dialectic can be applied to one’s inner struggle to discover their self-identity. This signifies that perhaps when consciousness is developed through enough social comparison, consciousness can become autonomous through the same dialectic within one’s mind. Whenever one is in a dilemma in decision-making, there are two streams of consciousness within one’s mind discussing what course of action to take. Thus, maybe a micro master-slave dialectic relationship can occur in one’s head, further supporting the idea that self-consciousness, though developed from birth through social recognition, can continue to form within one’s own mind. This means that self-consciousness is not always reliant on social interaction once a concept of self-identity is developed. Our self-identity continues to ebb and flow from birth to death because our own self-consciousness is consistently being built through conflicting perspectives, whether with otherness in the external world or within one’s own mind.

In conclusion, though the formation of self-consciousness is reliant on the perception of otherness in the social world and an unequal master-slave conflict, this may not mean that consciousness is an illusion that does not grant agency. In fact, perhaps recognition from otherness is the pathway to unlocking free will. So, perhaps this discussion does not dismiss the autonomy of consciousness because it cannot be formed without otherness, but maybe otherness is integral to creating the essential agent of consciousness that grants us free will.
