Build 3

Reconsolidation, Freedom, and Continuity

Ben Zemel

Honors 241

Build 3

In Build 1, I argued that the freed slave's attitude of a master was "borrowed" because it only existed as long as the master allowed it. After rereading, I think "borrowed" ignored what the freed slave does with his liberation once he has it. Fanon's line that the freed slave "went from one way of life to another, but not from one life to another" (Fanon 9) changed my thinking. His legal status changed, but his sense of self did not. That gap needs clearer terms. For Hegel and Fanon, consciousness is self-consciousness, a mind that exists “on his own account” (Hegel §196) and confirms this by finding itself in something outside it. Continuity means the link that a consciousness draws between who it used to be and who it is now. Reconsolidation is the process that changes that link by selecting past experiences, present circumstances, or future projections. Freedom is a status that can be handed over, as seen in the 1794 decree, or a freedom won through one’s own struggle.

What does reconsolidation look like when it works? Hegel’s servant first loses his continuity when fear of death shakes “everything solid and stable” in him “to its foundations” (Hegel §194). He rebuilds it from his work, through which consciousness "acquires an element of permanence" (Hegel §195). His work leaves lasting objects, and they become a past he can select from and reread. In the thing he shaped, consciousness “comes to see... its own independence” (Hegel §195), so work that looked like what is described as “an alienated existence” turns into where “he acquires a mind of his own” (Hegel §196). The labor does not change, but how he reads it does. However, effort alone does not produce this. Fear breaks and changes the old continuity, and work supplies the material for a new one.

Fanon's freed slave is missing that material. His freedom was given as a status, since "the black man was acted upon" (Fanon 9) and has no past of his own to select from. He "can find in his memory no trace of the struggle for liberty" (Fanon 9), and the only objects the event left behind, statues of "white France stroking the kinky hair" of the freed man (Fanon 9), record someone else's action. The "psychoses and sudden deaths" that came after this (Fanon 9) can be seen as a consciousness handed a new life that nothing in its past led to. So the freed slave turns to the present. He “wants the white man to turn on him and shout” so he can “show them” (Fanon 10), which is an attempt to create the struggle his past is missing. Fanon closes that option: "it is too late," and the French freed slave is "doomed to bite himself" (Fanon 10). With no one outside himself to struggle against, the slave’s struggle turns inward.

How can Fanon reject the past and then claim all of it? He writes that a fifteenth-century Black civilization “confers no patent of humanity on me” (Fanon 11), then says “what I have to recapture is the whole past of the world,” since he is “not responsible solely for the revolt in Santo Domingo” (Fanon 11). He refuses the racial limit that makes a Black man’s history only Black history, and replaces this descent with action as the test as he explains “every time a man has said no to an attempt to subjugate his fellows, I have felt solidarity with his act” (Fanon

11). That gives him the past of struggle his freed slave lacks. Looking forward, he asks whether his freedom was “given to me then in order to build the world of the You” (Fanon 12). Fanon accepts that his freedom was given but ties it to one he still has to win: “I am endlessly creating myself” (Fanon 12).

This changes my answer to Build 1. The freed slave’s attitude of a master is not borrowed but it lacks continuity. Together, the three cases show what reconsolidation needs. Hegel’s servant has fear and work to select from. The freed slave has no struggle of his own, and his present is “too late.” Fanon’s freedom was also given, but he selects a past through action instead of race. Fanon uses reconsolidation to argue that a freedom handed over becomes a person’s own only when consciousness connects it to action, which is why he calls for individuals to be “actional” (Fanon 10). The freed slave went from one way of life to another. Fanon shows what it takes to go from one life to another.
