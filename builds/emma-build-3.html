Build 3

Reconsolidation, Recognition, and Struggle

Emma Goldman

Hoffmann/Angeli

HONORS 241

9 October 2026

Build 3

In Build 1, I interpreted Fanon's account of withheld recognition as a situation imposed

from external perception. Rereading has made me think more deeply about the distinctions

between Fanon’s Black man and Hegel’s slave in their respective writings relating to

consciousness. I have come to the idea that Hegel’s master-slave dialectic goes beyond

questioning how recognition shapes consciousness, but also if consciousness itself can act on the

conditions that shape it. Additionally, I believe the master-slave struggle described by Hegel can

be understood through applying reconsolidation; he presents the struggle toward consciousness

as a process of reconsolidation.This is further complicated by Fanon, who argues that the

difference between owning one’s liberation and being granted it lies in mutual recognition, which

is not universally granted or applicable.

The course defines reconsolidation as altering one’s sense of “continuity” to select past

experiences, present circumstances, or future projections in order to navigate changes in who one

is. For Hegel, this is what consciousness attempts to become. A consciousness being “in-itself”

and “for-itself” articulates that the consciousness both is determinate and aware of itself as the

origin of the determination. Hegel suggests that this cannot be earned by recognition, but rather

when one places the responsibility within their own hands: “it is solely by staking one’s life that

freedom is won” (3). The risk of death must be assumed to illustrate that consciousness is pure

being for-itself, rather than adopted into existence. This effort of a struggle exemplifies how the

self’s past must be questioned and be rebuilt through efforts that may assume the risk of death,

akin to selecting or accepting the idea that future projections may affect who one is.

The master exhibits a failure to reconsolidate. While he may stake his life, the recognition

he receives is from what he regards as a subhuman consciousness, rendering his mutual

recognition inferior. His unwavering certainty of his consciousness inhibits the growth of self he

can undergo in questioning of his past, present, and future, preventing the reconsolidation of his

consciousness as a whole and leaving him in a rather stagnant state of self.

On the contrary, the slave is able to reconsolidate through resistance and struggle. Fear

dissolves “everything solid and stable” in him, which eliminates prior notions of continuity and

develops a need that labor is able to infinitely fulfill. His idea of labor as lasting gives him a

stronger basis for a new sense of self. However, ideas of his agency complicate this. Given that

fear is imposed upon him, but his labor is done by him, is the self he builds his own

consciousness, or a byproduct of fear imposed on him?

The master entirely derives his recognition from the slave, whereas the slave persistently

strengthens his own consciousness, enabling him to become less dependent on another overall.

This affects understanding of the struggle itself. If resistance is an expression of

self-consciousness, does consciousness cause the resistance, or is resistance the activity through

which consciousness appears? Reconsolidation suggests that perhaps the struggle is the occasion

on which a self gets reorganized out of what has happened to it.

Fanon complicates Hegel’s analysis by emphasizing that it rests on “an absolute

reciprocity,” and that when one “close[s] the circuit,” the other is kept “within himself” and

deprived “even of this being-for-itself” (7). Without movement in two directions, it is unclear

how the slave’s labor can return a self to him. The Black man’s continuity is reorganized by what

is done to him rather than by what he does. Fanon also suggests that his independence was

granted upon him rather than won: “The upheaval reached the Negroes from without. The Black

man was acted upon” (9). He adds that Negro knows nothing of the cost of freedom, for he has

not fought for it” (9).

Though reconsolidation may still occur, as the Black man evaluates a new, free self, does

it still contribute to a consciousness building one’s self if they themself do not pursue the effort?

Hegel’s servant can see himself in what he makes, but the same may not hold true for Fanon’s

Black man. If so, perhaps reconsolidation requires an aspect of ownership as well as change—the

individual must be doing the redefining and introspection of their past, present, and future. In its

absence, the process may still occur, but is the result instead a self shaped for him rather than by

him?

​ If consciousness only recognizes what its struggle has produced through reconsolidation,

was the struggle the cause of the self, or merely the occasion from which it was reconstructed?

Fanon complicates Hegel’s account by raising the question of whether consciousness can

actively participate in its own reconstruction when the circumstances that reshape it are imposed

from without.
