Build 3

Recognition, Narration, and Developing Consciousness

My initial understanding of Hegel’s work was heavily focused on the master-servant dialectic and the idea of two beings who were already distinct selves. As I saw it, the master and servant met, struggled, and were changed in regard to consciousness as a result. However, upon reading deeper, I realize that Hegel’s account does not start with two opposing selves; neither the master nor the servant is a confirmed self prior to their relationship. Rather, the relationship itself produces them as opposing poles of commanding and laboring. Hegel describes self-consciousness as “movement,” suggesting that consciousness cannot be understood as something that simply emerges and remains stagnant (Hegel 1). In this sense, narration offers a useful means of exploring consciousness: as the coordination of successive states through which a sense of self is gradually given clarity.

To begin, Hegel describes recognition as the condition through which self-consciousness actually exists. He asserts that "a self-consciousness exists for a self-consciousness" and that it exists only by being recognized by another (2). This recognition also does not occur all at once: each self-consciousness must pass through the other in order to return to itself, so that it "receives back its own self" only after having lost itself in the other (2). The self that returns is therefore not quite the self that departed, since it now carries the encounter within it. This makes consciousness resemble a narration: the self I understand in the present is not identical to the self that first encounters an other, yet the two cannot be separated into entirely different selves either. Consciousness, then, may depend on connecting differences across successive states rather than possessing a stable identity at every moment.

However, presenting consciousness as a sequence complicates how I previously understood labor. In Build 1, I viewed the master's control and the servant's work as causes of discipline, and discipline as what awakened self-consciousness. Hegel, though, does not seem to present these moments in a chronological chain. He describes fear, service, and formative activity as all “necessary” (5), illustrating what occurs when one is missing: without formative activity, fear “remains inward and mute” (5), yet work without fear becomes an “empty self-centered attitude of accomplishment” (6). These moments, thus, cannot independently account for the development of consciousness. Rather, narration becomes critical here as it emphasizes how a state gains significance through its place within a larger sequence, rather than treating a singular state as the independent cause of consciousness.

Hegel further explains that through work, the servant “becomes conscious of what he truly is” (5), coming to see his own independence in the object he has formed. From this later position, work that initially appeared to be mere obedience can also be understood as part of the servant's recognition of independence. Yet, it’s important to acknowledge that the servant's recognition of independence occurs within a relationship of dependence, not outside it. His activity contributes to his developing self-consciousness, even though he does not independently determine the conditions under which he works. This underscores a tension, then, between the relational formation of consciousness and the servant's active participation in that development.

Fanon’s account of emancipation as freedom complicates this further by testing whether changing states of being necessarily amount to a development of consciousness. Describing the French slave’s emancipation by white owners, Fanon affirms that he “went from one way of life to another, but not from one life to another” (Fanon 9). The change in state is real, but the later state of freedom is granted externally rather than won through the slave’s own struggle. Reading through narration, this raises a problem: although an earlier and later state exist, their chronological succession does not necessarily make the transition a meaningful development. This does not mean that a being must control every circumstance that shapes them, but it raises the question of what role their own activity plays in making change part of their development of consciousness.

That is, if the self is continually shaped through encounters and events that are not entirely within its control, what does it mean for consciousness to actively narrate its own development? Within Hegel and Fanon’s frameworks, narration may not require full autonomy over one’s development. Instead, it may involve the ability to relate earlier states to the present and reinterpret what they have come to mean, even if this ability is itself formed in relationship. In that sense, consciousness may be understood as neither a fixed sense of self nor a succession of changing states, but as the continually developing connection between them. Whether consciousness must direct this process for it to count as autonomous, or whether actively participating in it is enough, may be a question the readings leave open.
