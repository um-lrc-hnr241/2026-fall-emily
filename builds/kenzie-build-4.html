An Objection of Phenomenality in Hegel-Fanon: Relationality Dismissing Individual Experience?

An Objection of Phenomenality in Hegel-Fanon: Relationality Dismissing Individual

Experience? In Build #3, I had previously argued that the process of engaging in Hegel’s master-slave dialectic was inherently a phenomenal experience because, although this conflict relies on mutual recognition, neither consciousness can internalize the other’s individual experience. In other words, this is an inward, undefined experience that can only be lived through a first-person perspective. Yet, one may ask: can a first-person perspective exist without the third-person recognition of other consciousness? Hegel claims that “self-consciousness is in fact the reflection out of the world of sense and perception and is essentially the return from otherness” (Hegel 167). Immediately, Hegel claims that our sense of self is dependent on our relations to others.

This means that perhaps our sense of self is more fragile than we comprehend it. Would the sense of self exist if there were no comparison of it to another? If this is not the case, is the experience of phenomenality a valid concept? Can we truly experience something indescribable to another if phenomenality relies on otherness to exist? Perhaps the master-slave dialectic may not be a phenomenal experience at all: our development of the self may actually be less autonomous in its development than we assume. Despite these questions, Hegel-Fanon still argues that the development of self- consciousness cannot occur by mutual recognition alone.

Specifically, in Fanon, it is noted that “the White master, without conflict, recognized the Negro slave” (Fanon 7). He claims this diminished any sense of true, experienced freedom that the French slave could possess, as he still “wants to make himself recognized” (Fanon 7). Specifically, Fanon emphasizes “human reality in itself-for-itself can be achieved only through conflict and through risk that conflict implies… transformation of subjective certainty of my own worth into universally valid objective truth” (Fanon 8). This explanation demonstrates that although the master-slave dialectic is dependent on recognition of otherness, that does not diminish the individual experience of both consciousnesses. As Hegel describes, both consciousnesses are “mutually recognizing each other” (Hegel 184), yet one cannot describe to the other exactly how it feels to recognize the other. If both consciousnesses felt the same feeling of recognition of another consciousness, there would be no attempt at engagement with unhappy consciousness, the constant struggle of recognition and negation of otherness.

One could also argue that since Hegel is able to define this experience, the master-slave dialectic is empirical, reducing its phenomenality. He describes, “First, it has lost itself, for it finds itself in another being; secondly, in doing so, it has superseded the other, for it does not see the other as an essential being, but in the other sees its own self” (Hegel 180). This quote may suggest that since this process can be defined procedurally, this experience is less phenomenal than I had previously assumed. Yet, it is important to examine the thought experiment of Mary’s room. Created by philosopher Frank Jackson, Mary knows every aspect of seeing color empirically, but once she leaves the monochromatic room, she learns the experience of seeing color that could not be defined on paper. This demonstrates that Hegel’s ideas, though capable of being described, cannot capture the true experience of the conflict of an autonomous self versus the dependence of establishing self from another.

This process must go through work, which requires “desire [to be] held in check, fleetingness staved off” (194). If the struggle of understanding the self without the other and work is not achieved, Fanon explains that the self- consciousness ends up incomplete, becoming a form of “thinghood” (Hegel 189). Therefore, Hegel may be attempting to create some sense of objectivity by explaining the master-slave dialectic. However, even explaining the master-slave dialectic in his writing is as contradictory as his idea of Unhappy Consciousness because he attempts to explain an inherently phenomenal process. This means that, like Mary, one can empirically understand his description of the master-slave dialectic, but cannot actually develop self-consciousness without experiencing this process, making the master-slave dialectic phenomenal. Thus, one can argue that the master-slave dialectic is contradictory to the concept of phenomenality because it relies on shared consciousness rather than just oneself’s experience.

However, this contradictory experience is inherently phenomenal because, though it relies on mutual recognition of otherness, the desire, labour, and development of the self are lived individually by each consciousness. And though this process can be explained, Hegel understands that his explanation must leave room for the reader to subjectively experience the objective process described by him.
