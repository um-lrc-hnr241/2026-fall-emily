Build 5

Hegel, the Gita, and the Narrating Self

Both Hegel and the Gita present self-understanding as something consciousness must arrive at, rather than something immediately possessed. For Hegel this requires recognition by another self; for Krishna it requires Arjuna to reconsider his identification with action. In both, the individual’s initial understanding of themself is incomplete. This resembles narration as I’ve understood it thus far: not necessarily a story, but the coordination of successive states through which a sense of self is given clarity. Yet if the self interpreting its development is shaped by relations and forces beyond its control, who or what is doing the narrating?

The authors differ in what consciousness must recognize beyond its initial perspective. For Hegel, another self-consciousness must remain independent; the other cannot simply be absorbed into the self’s understanding. The Gita, by contrast, describes a disciplined person who looks impartially upon “lover, friend or foe” (Gita 6.9) and “sees the same self everywhere” (6.32). It directs Arjuna toward Atman, an essential self not confined to his individual ego. Hegel emphasizes the relation between distinct consciousnesses, while the Gita emphasizes a sameness underlying their apparent differences.

Hegel’s account of recognition suggests that relationships are not experiences added to an already-formed self. He describes an encounter with an other as a “double movement” in which each consciousness’s action is also the action of the other (Hegel 3). Each becomes a “middle term” through which the other returns to itself (3). The other is not merely someone who influences an existing self; the relationship helps constitute the positions from which each understands itself. This complicates narration: the perspective relating earlier and later states is itself formed through relations with others. The narrating consciousness, then, cannot look back as though it had independently produced the self that now interprets its past.

The Gita questions the authority of the interpreting self differently. Arjuna initially sees the battle through its consequences: he will kill relatives, teachers, and friends, and considers withdrawing. Krishna does not ask him to avoid action; rather, he instructs Arjuna to fulfill his duty without selfishness or attachment to its results (Gita 3.8, 3.30). He challenges the ego's belief that “I am the actor” (3.27). By explaining that actions arise from the qualities of nature, Krishna separates participation in action from the assumption that the individual ego is its ultimate source. His statement that “the self never acts” (13.29) further distinguishes the essential self from the individual who claims ownership of its actions. Arjuna must still fight, but he need not understand his participation as an expression of an independent self that authors its own conduct. Looking back on the battle, he might therefore understand his actions as the fulfillment of duty rather than as proof of his own agency. Where Hegel emphasizes the other consciousness external to the self as necessary to self-understanding, the Gita questions whether the ego's identification with action allows the essential self to be understood at all.

Yet, beneath these differences lies a shared challenge to the self as the independent author of its development. For Hegel, the self depends on another consciousness for self-understanding; for the Gita, the ego mistakes itself for the source of actions arising through nature. Both make the individual’s claim to independence uncertain. This develops my understanding of narration: connecting successive states is not enough if the one doing the relating is treated as a fixed origin outside the process it describes. Narration must also examine how that perspective has been formed and where its claims to agency may be mistaken.

Yet, how can narration allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality and influence its course? It may do so not by placing consciousness outside causality, but by making past experience available for reflection before another action occurs. Arjuna initially wants to withdraw, but Krishna’s teaching gives him a different way to understand the impending action, and Arjuna ultimately fights. The battlefield remains, but the interpretation guiding his response changes. When consciousness relates an immediate impulse to remembered relationships, previous consequences, and possible future actions, alternatives become available. That reflection may alter a decision, which then becomes a cause of subsequent events. Narration may therefore offer agency in some form: not necessarily control over every condition that forms the self, but participation in determining what follows.
