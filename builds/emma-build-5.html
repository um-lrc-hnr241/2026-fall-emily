Hegel, Scarry, and Reconsolidation

Emma Goldman

Angeli/Hoffmann

HONORS241

6 November 2026

Build 5

Both Hegel and Scarry explore consciousness’s quality of reconsolidation, or the

alteration of one’s sense of continuity to select past experiences, present circumstances, or future

projections in order to navigate changes in who one is. Both authors present consciousness as

taking part in reconstructing itself through its interaction with the external world. For Hegel, the

slave’s experience of fear leaves “everything solid and stable . . . shaken to its foundations”

(Hegel 5), while for Scarry, pain overwhelms the person because it lacks direct expression,

leaving only “the elemental ‘as if’ of the person in pain” (Scarry 279). Rather than remaining

passive in response to these disruptions, consciousness acts upon the external world to give form

to an altered understanding of itself.

The authors differ in how consciousness exercises this capacity. For Hegel,

reconsolidation begins through an experience of fear and dependence that the slave does not

choose, forcing him to alter his perception of past dependence to establish continuity. In shaping

an external object, he recognizes his own independence embodied in it: “consciousness, as a

worker, comes to see in the independent being [of the object] its own independence” (Hegel 5).

However, his independence remains complicated by the fact that his labor is initiated and

directed by the master. This raises the question of whether consciousness can be independent

through actions it does not originate. Hegel’s account suggests that it can, in a limited way. The

slave does not initiate the process that transforms him, yet he participates in it by giving form to

the world. For Hegel, then, reconsolidation changes the self: the slave’s understanding of himself

is rebuilt around what he can do.

Scarry, by contrast, locates the change in the experience rather than the self. Through

projection, “the original given is utterly eliminated and replaced by something wholly other than

itself” (Scarry 285). The individual attempts to gain “some share of the blissful immunity of inert

inanimate objecthood,” while the object is deprived of “its immunity to . . . the problems of

sentience” (Scarry 285). Projection thus displaces an experience onto an object. The artifact,

however, preserves it: “though it [the artifact] cannot be sentiently aware of pain, it is in the

essential fact of itself the objectification of that awareness; . . . its design, its structure is the

structure of a perception” (Scarry 289). Unlike Hegel’s slave, Scarry’s maker needs no new

self-understanding; continuity is carried instead by what is made. Does the artifact change the

maker, or simply make private experience visible?

The authors also differ in the role another consciousness plays in this process. Although

the slave develops his independence through labor, this relationship with the master lacks

reciprocal recognition: “. . . a form of recognition has arisen that is one-sided and unequal”

(Hegel 4). Scarry, however, expands reconsolidation into an intersubjective process, presenting

external objects as creating the possibility of extending individual experience beyond its maker:

“. . . [the maker] enters into and in some way alters the alive percipience of other persons”

(Scarry 307). This difference matters for reconsolidation itself: Hegel’s slave seems to confirm

that reconsolidation is only a change within one mind, while Scarry suggests it can reach others

through the objects that carry it.

Yet, beneath these differences lies a common insight, namely that reconsolidation

requires consciousness to give its experience a form outside itself. In Build 4, I treated

reconsolidation as a reorganization within consciousness; comparing Hegel and Scarry suggests

that continuity is carried by what is made. Giving form therefore does not mean choosing the

change, since neither the slave’s fear nor the maker’s pain is chosen. Rather, it means working

with what has already happened to them. Reconsolidation thus suggests that consciousness does

not exercise complete control over its development, but neither is it simply shaped by forces

outside itself. Whether that form is recognized by others, however, lies partly beyond the

maker’s control.

Hegel and Scarry both use reconsolidation in order to show how consciousness develops

a new understanding of itself through experiences that initially exceed its control. Yet, how can

reconsolidation allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality and

influence its course? The slave’s fear is imposed on him, and left alone it would keep him

dependent. Recognizing what he can do lets him reinterpret his past dependence, so his response

draws on possibilities the fear alone did not contain. Scarry’s artifact similarly adds a new cause

to the world by changing how others perceive. Fear and pain still set the terms for the labor and

the artifact, so reconsolidation suggests influence rather than exemption; consciousness redirects

a causal chain by giving what happens to it a form that later events must respond to.
