Build 4

Fanon, Reconsolidation, and a Chosen Past

Ben Zemel Honors 241 Build 4

In Build 3, I argued that Fanon uses reconsolidation to show that a handed-over freedom becomes a person’s own only when consciousness connects it to action. Reconsolidation is the process of changing the link a consciousness draws between who it was and who it is now, by selecting past experiences, present circumstances, or future projections. Hegel’s servant rebuilds that link from his work, through which consciousness “acquires an element of permanence” (Hegel §195). His work leaves lasting objects, and they become a past he can select from. Fanon’s freed slave cannot do the same, because he “can find in his memory no trace of the struggle for liberty” (Fanon 9). Fanon himself succeeds by selecting a past through action instead of race when he says, “every time a man has said no to an attempt to subjugate his fellows, I have felt solidarity with his act” (Fanon 11).

However, one might object that reconsolidation does not fit Fanon’s conception of consciousness because Fanon rejects the idea that the past determines what he can become. He says, “Like it or not, the past can in no way guide me in the present moment” (Fanon 11). He describes himself as arriving without history at all: “I find myself suddenly in the world” (Fanon 12). In this reading, the freed slave’s problem is not that he lacks a past of his own struggle, it’s that he is always looking for one. He “wants the white man to turn on him and shout” (Fanon 10) so he can have the fight he never got to have. This leaves him waiting on the white man, and “most often there is nothing—nothing but indifference, or a paternalistic curiosity” (Fanon 10). If that is true, Fanon’s answer is to stop looking backward, and reconsolidation is what the freed slave gets wrong.

On the other hand, if one looks more closely at which past Fanon rejects, the objection covers only half of his move. The first half is refusing the past that race gives him. A Negro civilization in the fifteenth century “confers no patent of humanity on me” (Fanon 11) because it would make his history a racial inheritance he did not choose. The second half is replacing it. He later explains, “what I have to recapture is the whole past of the world” (Fanon 11). So what Fanon rejects is the past race assigns him, and he replaces it with a past he chooses through action.

The objection still changes my claim, because “suddenly” and “endlessly” show a difference between Fanon and Hegel that I did not see in Build 3. Hegel’s servant reconsolidates through fear and work, and the result is “permanence” (Hegel §195). The thing he shaped stays, and consciousness “comes to see in the independent being [of the object] its own independence” (Hegel §195). This shows how the servant had an ability he did not know he had: “Through this rediscovery of himself by himself,” he finds that in his work “he acquires a mind of his own” (Hegel §196). Fanon’s chosen past does not have an object like that. “Suddenly” means he arrives with no finished history to look back at. “Endlessly,” from the line I used in Build 3, “I am endlessly creating myself” (Fanon 12), means that creating never ends. His solidarity forms “every time” a man says no (Fanon 11). Since this past is built from actions and not from race or an object, Fanon has to choose it again each time. In this case, self-creation is still reconsolidation for Fanon, because he creates his continuity by choosing which past to claim.

Fanon uses reconsolidation to argue that a freedom handed over becomes a person’s own through action, and the continuity that action produces is never complete. Fanon gives up permanence for a continuity he has to always be remaking, which is why he wants to “educate man to be actional” (Fanon 10).

However, if one considers how Fanon uses reconsolidation in order to make a freedom he was given his own, one can begin to see how reconsolidation might allow consciousness to step into a chain of physical causes and change its outcome. On its own, the 1794 decree led to “psychoses and sudden deaths” (Fanon 9). Without a past to select from, the freed slave can only react, and Fanon says “there is always resentment in a reaction” (Fanon 10). Fanon cannot change what happened in 1794, but by choosing which past to act from, he can change what it leads to.
