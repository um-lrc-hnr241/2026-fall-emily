Build 4

Narration and the Meaning of Transformation

In Build 3, I suggested that narration offers a way to understand how consciousness develops by connecting successive states whose significance emerges through their relation to one another. I also proposed that consciousness actively participates in this development, even if it cannot control all the interactions it has. But one might object that narration doesn’t fit Hegel’s conception of consciousness because it attributes an organizing ability to consciousness itself. Hegel describes a dialectical movement through which self-consciousness develops, not a self that reflects on its experiences and turns them into a story. Indeed, although fear is “the beginning of wisdom,” Hegel explains that consciousness is not yet aware of its being-for-itself (5). The servant only “becomes conscious of what he truly is” through work (5). His development thus precedes his understanding of it. If the servant does not recognize the significance of his experiences as he undergoes them, then narration may describe how an observer organizes the servant’s development rather than act as a quality of consciousness.

Still, this objection risks treating Hegel’s development as a random sequence of events lacking clear relation to one another. Hegel insists that fear, service, and formative activity are all “necessary” (5) in the servant’s development of consciousness. Without work, fear “remains inward and mute,” while work without fear produces an “empty self-centered attitude of accomplishment” (5-6). These moments are relevant because of their relationships to one another, not simply because one follows another. Work gives expression to what fear alone cannot accomplish, while fear gives work a significance it would otherwise lack. Hegel’s account therefore has a recognizable pattern in which each moment gains significance through the development of the others. This supports a narrative reading of his account, although it does not establish that consciousness itself narrates this process.

The servant’s eventual recognition complicates this distinction. Through his work, he undergoes a “rediscovery of himself by himself” (5). His activity produces an object in which he comes to recognize his own independence, allowing him to understand what his work has made possible. This recognition is not merely an observer’s interpretation imposed on an otherwise unrelated series of events; the servant comes to recognize himself in his own formative activity. Yet he does so retroactively, without having understood its significance in advance. Hegel’s narration, then, may not require consciousness to actively organize its experiences as they occur. It might instead describe how consciousness retroactively recognizes the significance of its earlier activity. However, this possibility raises a further question: is such recognition enough to make a transition part of consciousness’s development, or can one still mistake mere change for transformation?

Fanon explores this problem by showing how one can mistake a change in circumstances for a development of consciousness. He describes a former French slave who receives freedom from white masters without having fought for it. The upheaval brings values that “had not been created by his actions,” as the slave moves “from one way of life to another, but not from one life to another” (Fanon 9). Fanon is not simply arguing that freedom matters only when won through individual struggle. His description of the former slave as one “allowed to assume the attitude of a master” (9) suggests that emancipation changes his position without transforming the hierarchical relationship through which recognition is granted. One could narrate this transition as progress because one state follows another, but that sequence alone does not establish that a meaningful transformation has occurred. This returns to the opening objection: an observer may impose a pattern of development on events without accounting for what has actually changed in the consciousness being examined.

Narration in Hegel’s framework, may be less a conscious activity explicitly assigned to the self than as a way of examining when a sequence of states becomes a development of consciousness. Fanon, however, warns that even a coherent story of progress can conceal the persistence of the relationships that shape consciousness. Yet if one considers how Hegel presents the servant’s eventual recognition of his own independence through work, one might see how narration might allow consciousness to intervene in its own development by reinterpreting what its past activity signifies. Narration may therefore also help distinguish a meaningful transformation that consciousness comes to recognize from one that an observer merely assumes has occurred. Yet if consciousness must interpret its development through relationships that have already shaped its understanding of itself, what allows it to recognize the limits of that interpretation? In this sense, narration’s significance may lie in how it leaves the relationship between one’s past and present open to question.
