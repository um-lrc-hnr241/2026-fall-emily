Hegel and Scarry: The Contradiction of Phenomenality and the Desire for Recognition

Hegel and Scarry: The Contradiction of Phenomenality and the Desire for Recognition

Both Scarry and Hegel understand that phenomenality exists despite the influential role of the external world in developing the self. Yet, both authors differ in how they define this external world and the self’s relationship with it. Scarry describes the self as projecting its internal experiences onto non-sentient objects to externalize its phenomenal experiences. Hegel, conversely, describes the self as developing through its relationship with another independent consciousness. Thus, while the self in Scarry develops by engaging with subjective reciprocation when projecting onto objects, Hegelian self-consciousness can only exist through the struggle of attempting independence while recognizing otherness.

Scarry’s work The Body of Pain describes a human as a “projecting creature” (284) that projects to gain “some small share of blissful immunity of inert inanimate objecthood” while the external world is deprived of this immunity and experiences the “problems of sentience” (285). Therefore, Scarry's process of projection demonstrates that the self engages in a relationship with non-sentient objects to externally express its phenomenal experiences. Scarry diverges from Hegel, however, by lacking the mutual recognition aspect of the relationship. These objects are recipients of projection rather than as a force that can reciprocate back onto the self. Thus, any form of reciprocation of consciousness from projection in Scarry largely originates from one’s own imagination. In Hegel, however, this reciprocation of consciousness is less imagined as the other consciousness demands the same recognition as the self.

This creates the fundamental difference in the level of recognition in Scarry versus Hegel. In Scarry, since the recipient of projected consciousness is an object, a self-consciousness does not have to re-develop itself based on the struggle of recognition because the object cannot “experience discomfort nor recognize discomfort in others” (289). Instead, this allows humans to engage in the creation or manipulation of objects based on “the projection of the ‘awareness of aliveness’”(289). For example, if a human experiences deeply phenomenal pain, they have the agency to project such feelings onto objects without struggle and retaliation. For example, the Korean tree “at the very least, would have given a signal… as civilized trees… routinely do” (Scarry 289), instead of ‘letting the soldiers die’. The soldiers' treatment of the tree demonstrates how humans can externalize their phenomenal experiences without the burden of the master- slave struggle. Conversely, the self in Hegel does not have the liberty of projecting completely onto a non-sentient being without conflict arising from the fact that each consciousness recognizes and is affected by the other. If Hegel describes consciousness as “essentially the return from otherness” (Hegel 167), then the Hegelian self’s level of reciprocation is dependent on mutual recognition. So, the self in Hegel cannot express its inner world through projection without being dependent on the reactions of otherness, as each two consciousnesses interacting “recognize themselves as mutually recognizing each other” (184). Thus, self-consciousness attempts to assert independence despite the fact that the self is dependent on otherness. This demonstrates something beyond Scarry’s idea of imaginary conscious relationships with objects, but causal interactions that shape the two self-consciousness after the master-slave dialectic, meaning that the self in Hegel is less of a solely inner-world identity, but more of a distinct yet ever-changing identity shaped by interactions with its external environment.

Therefore, both of these authors concur that the phenomenality of self-consciousness is dependent on its external environment. This leads to the essential contradiction of self- consciousness: if the self is commonly understood as being aware of one’s existence as an independent entity, why do both authors discuss that the self asks for recognition, whether through projection onto objects or the master-slave dialectic with another? Perhaps the main contradiction of self-consciousness, that actually demonstrates that consciousness exists, is that we seek out to be recognized on a phenomenal level, despite the impossibility of fully communicating our individual experiences.
