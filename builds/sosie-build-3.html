Quality of Consciousness: Deliberation

Quality of Consciousness: Deliberation

After reading the passage again, I understand that in Build 1 I framed my question too narrowly. I asked whether self-consciousness causes anything at all, as if the only options were that it does or doesn’t. I’ve come to realize that self-consciousness is not this simple; I overlooked that Fanon contrasts two kinds of causation. Saying that the black man was “acted upon” describes a change caused by outside factors. The alternative, “the Negro battles and is battled,” describes someone deliberating: weighing the outcomes of submitting, resisting, or risking death, and choosing one. Nothing about this is automatic. Unlike a rock, this person’s path is not fixed by the forces acting on them. So the better question is not whether self-consciousness causes action, but how struggle changes a person from someone who is pushed around by outside forces into someone who weighs options and chooses.

Hegel’s claim that “it is solely by staking one’s life that freedom is won” helps answer this question. To stake your own life you must consider at least two possible outcomes: submit and live unfree, and resist and risk death. Both arise from the same circumstances, and although the pressure is enormous, neither is determined the way a rock’s path is. The person has to reason about them, and that is what separates deliberation from automatic reaction. A rock rolling down a hill has no alternative scenarios, but someone choosing whether they should risk everything for freedom does. On Hegel’s account, self-consciousness is not a feeling that appears after the struggle, but it develops through the deliberative act of facing death and choosing otherwise.

This also highlights what the freed slaves were missing. Enslaved people deliberated constantly, weighing whether resisting or complying would cost them their lives, so what the freed slaves lacked was not deliberation in general but deliberation over freedom itself. Freedom reached them as an announcement, so they never had to stake their lives to win it. They did not have to picture themselves risking death in a fight for freedom versus staying enslaved, because someone had settled it for them. This helps explain why Fanon records "psychoses and sudden deaths": they were handed a result without any say or choice. This fits Hegel's view that recognition between people, a mutual self-consciousness, cannot be given as a gift. It requires a direct confrontation in which each side risks something. The best evidence is the freed slave who wishes the white man would insult him so that he could fight back. His imagined confrontation looks like an attempt to recreate the struggle he was denied in the hope that it will give him the self-consciousness his granted freedom alone did not.

This all changes how I would answer my open-ended question in Build one: does struggle cause self-consciousness, does self-consciousness cause action, or does struggle cause both? Deliberation suggests the answer is none of these. Struggle forces a person to reason between alternatives, and that deliberation produces both the chosen action and the self-consciousness, so neither causes the other. Instead of self-consciousness being something totally separate that later triggers action, it develops through the reasoning by which action is chosen. That is why Fanon’s freed slaves are “acted upon” rather than agents of their own freedom, even though their lives enormously changed. An epiphenomenalist could still argue that deliberation is merely a byproduct, with the real work happening underneath. Fanon’s example cannot rule that out, but it does make something harder to explain: if deliberating did nothing, why would the absence of deliberation cause such damage and make the freed slave crave it? It doesn’t prove that self-consciousness causes action, but it shows that the difference between being caused and choosing is something people feel and experience. What it does show is that the difference between being caused and choosing matters to people, and any account that treats deliberation as idle has to explain why its absence is so devastating.
