Build 1

Borrowed Freedom and Recognition

Ben Zemel–Honors 241 Build 1

Chosen Passage: "The Negro is a slave who has been allowed to assume the attitude of a master. The white man is a master who has allowed his slaves to eat at his table" (Fanon 9).​

A homeowner leaving town hands their keys to a guest, who is told to make themselves at home, sit where they like, and eat whatever they want. The house is not theirs, but for as long as the owner allows, the guest is in charge. However, the guest’s behavior never fully matches the owner’s. When the owner returns, the guest’s run ends. Frantz Fanon describes a similar concept when he writes that the newly freed slave “has been allowed to assume the attitude of the master” (Fanon 9). The word attitude is not describing a feeling, but a way of carrying yourself that was not earned. Fanon is not describing an alteration in who an individual actually is. He is describing a change in how they are allowed to carry themselves. This idea poses a question relating to consciousness itself: if a person can carry themselves as free, equal, in control, and powerful, but only because another person recognizes them that way, is that consciousness theirs? Or does it belong to whoever is granting the recognition? Consciousness itself might be nothing more than a role performed convincingly enough, by both the person recognized and the one granting the recognition, to pass as real. Hegel claims that true independent self-consciousness must be earned through labor or through staking one’s life in what he calls the fear of death, “the absolute Master” (Hegel §194). However, almost no one actually does this. Hegel splits self-consciousness into two positions: one “whose essential nature is simply to live, or to be, for another” and one “whose essential nature is to be for itself” (Hegel §189). Fanon’s sentence is structured the same way. The freed slave is “allowed to assume” a role, while the white man “has allowed him” to eat at his table (Fanon 9). Hegel’s depiction of living for another connects directly to Fanon’s explanation of a freed slave obtaining a new label and attitude. However, the position underneath remains the dependent one. Even though the house was never his, the guest lives the same life as the owner while the permission lasts. Hegel’s argument develops past the guest’s side of the exchange. He argues that the owner’s claim to the house depends on another individual recognizing him as the owner, since mastery “proves the reverse of what it wants to be” (Hegel §193): the position that appears as independent is revealed to need someone else’s recognition to hold any meaning at all. This reversal suggests that no position of authority is ever truly independent. It exists only because someone else agrees to treat it as real. If the owner needs the guest to treat him as the one in charge for his ownership to count as real, then his position is “for another” and not “for itself.” In the passage, the white man is only a master because there is a slave present for him to allow at his table. The question strays from whether the freed slave’s attitude of a master is real or not. It becomes whether the master’s own mastery was ever more than the same borrowed arrangement the freed slave is in.

The explored question has shifted twice. It began as whether the freed slave’s new attitude and freedom count as real consciousness and became whether the master’s claim of power was ever more than the same borrowed arrangement the slave is in. If neither position stands fully independent or “for itself”, if the slave depends on being recognized by the master and the master depends on being recognized by the slave, then maybe there is nothing in the passage that is unborrowed. That could mean the freed slave never had real consciousness to begin with, or that living inside a role granted by someone else is the only kind of consciousness anyone achieves. Both slave and master alike gain freedoms through recognition carried convincingly enough to function as real. Neither the slave nor the master earned the position of autonomy and power over their decisions through labor or through facing death the way Hegel demands, yet both live inside it as though it were real. If that is the case, is borrowed consciousness any less verified than the self-consciousness Hegel describes? Or perhaps, no consciousness was ever anything more than borrowed to begin with.
