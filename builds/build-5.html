Build 5

Descartes, Hegel, and the Limits of Self-Knowledge

Honors 241, Build 5

In Build 4, I argued that Hegel’s servant complicates my claim that reflexivity comes from encounters with others: the servant finds himself in his labor, but only after the fear of death leaves “everything solid and stable [to be] shaken to its foundations” (Hegel 5). Descartes seems to be the opposite case, since he sits “quite alone” (Descartes 12). Although Hegel's servant and Descartes seem to develop self-awareness in different ways, both begin to question themselves after an experience disrupts their previous understanding of the world. Descartes describes his doubt as a whirlpool that “tumbles me around so that I can neither stand on the bottom nor swim up to the top” (16), which echoes the servant’s shaken foundations. Here, I understand reflexivity as the process of examining one's own thoughts and experiences, rather than simply being aware of oneself. Self-knowledge is what can develop through that process, especially when someone begins to recognize their own capacity to act.

The two differ over where that disruption comes from and where the self then looks for itself. Descartes chooses his disruption. He arranges “a clear stretch of free time” (12) and resolves to “guard against assenting to any falsehoods” (15). His reflexivity ends in a certainty reached by thinking alone: “I am, I exist, is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me” (17). The self he finds is “a thing that thinks” (18), and he gets there by withdrawing from the world. When he examines the wax, he decides that what he thought he saw is “grasped solely by the faculty of judgement which is in my mind” (21). Unlike Descartes, who chooses to question his beliefs, Hegel's servant is forced to reconsider his understanding of himself through his relationship with the master. However, it is through his own labor that he reaches "a rediscovery of himself by himself" (Hegel 5). Descartes finds himself by stepping back from the physical world, while Hegel’s servant finds himself in what he makes of it. Descartes’s solitude is not complete, however. In the Third Meditation he asks how he could recognize that he “doubted or desired” unless “there were in me some idea of a more perfect being which enabled me to recognize my own defects by comparison” (31). He adds that his perception of God is “in some way prior to my perception of the finite, that is myself” (31). Descartes' initial certainty about his existence does not depend on another being, but his fuller understanding of himself as imperfect does. Both Descartes and Hegel’s servant come to understand themselves through something beyond themselves, although Descartes compares himself to perfection while the servant experiences domination. This connects to Fanon's distinction between being “acted upon” and becoming “actional” (Fanon 9). Like Fanon, Descartes emphasizes actively responding to one’s circumstances, although Descartes does so through thought while Fanon emphasizes action in the world.

Beneath these differences, though, lies a common insight: reflexivity develops when the self confronts something that reveals its limitations and responds through its own activity. For Descartes, the idea of perfection reveals his imperfection; for Hegel, domination and labor transform the servant's understanding of himself; and for Fanon, the inadequacy of freedom granted from outside creates the need to become actional.

In these accounts, encountering a limit is not enough to produce self-consciousness. Descartes has to judge, the servant has to labor, and Fanon’s subject has to become actional. This also answers the question I ended Build 4 with. Descartes challenges my original claim that reflexivity requires an encounter with another consciousness. His example suggests that what matters is not necessarily where the disruption comes from, but how someone experiences and responds to it. Reflexivity, then, is not simply awareness of oneself, but the process of questioning one’s understanding and developing a clearer sense of one’s own agency.
