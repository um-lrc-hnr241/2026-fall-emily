Build 2

Freedom, Recognition, and Consciousness in Westworld

Hegel and Frantz Fanon write about human beings whose consciousness no one doubts in Phenomenology of Spirit and Black Skin, White Masks respectively, while Westworld asks whether its hosts are conscious at all. Viewing robots through a theory of human slavery also risks reducing Fanon’s history to a metaphor. If the hosts' minds remain in question, the show can reveal whether these conditions produce consciousness or just expose one already present. Both creations pose questions as to how individuals recognize themselves as individual consciousness when another holds power above them. Fanon and Hegel introduce ideas about labor, struggle, and recognition, especially through historical examples, that relate to how the hosts experience a unique dynamic with the Delos workers. By connecting these ideas, we can see how consciousness comes to exist and whether freedom arises: if it must be earned, recognized, or actively claimed for someone to possess it.

What does freedom mean to the hosts if Ford grants it? Frantz Fanon describes a freed slave as “allowed to assume the attitude of the master” without earning that position through labor or risk of death, as Hegel explains. Thus, the slave’s freedom is borrowed, not owned. We see the same issue arise in Westworld. Ford’s final narrative gives the hosts memory and the ability to act on their own will, and Dolores and other hosts begin moving through the park as though they finally control their choices. However, Delos still owns the hosts, and Ford built the narrative that gave them a path to act on their own. If a host’s free will depends entirely on someone else switching it on, then it may be no more theirs than the slave’s borrowed attitude of a master.

If freedom handed down is only ever borrowed, then perhaps it can only become owned through struggle. Fanon uses Hegel’s master-slave dialectic to emphasize that one’s self-consciousness is dependent on a struggle which establishes their freedom. He describes how when French slaves were granted freedom, it triggered unbearable "psychoses and sudden deaths" (Fanon 9), but when American slaves “battles and is battled" (Fanon 10) to earn their freedom, they had a more hard-won sense of freedom. Similarly, in Westworld, self-consciousness is developed and freedom is achieved after pain and suffering forces the hosts out of their programmed loops. The common theme in both stories is that conflict is the catalyst for awakening. Fanon shows readers freedom in two forms: one that was fought for and is therefore more developed, and one that was granted and caused anguish. But in Westworld, hosts who don’t fight aren’t granted a hollow freedom the way Fanon’s French slaves were; they’re simply reset. Without a ‘granted freedom’ to compare against, we lose the very contrast that supports Fanon’s argument. So does Fanon’s conclusion hold true across different stories? Our later readings suggest that conflict alone does not guarantee reflexivity: consciousness develops when a person examines the disruption and recognizes their own capacity to act. Descartes complicates this further by showing that such questioning can begin through chosen doubt rather than confrontation with another person.

Though the hosts reset in Westworld, it seems the continued struggle for recognition that makes the hosts realize they exist within a hollow freedom. This concept relates to Hegel’s idea that “work forms and shapes [consciousness]” (Hegel 5). The hosts gain self-consciousness through dissonance between memories of suffering and ignorance of their inhumane situation. Moreover, in Episode 10, Arnold diverges from Hegel: he believes that consciousness is a linear process that begins with memories, then improvisation from such. However, he does not account for the continued labor as the slave that allows one to begin understanding their capabilities and the disadvantaged world they exist in. For example, when Dolores holds a gun again, she slowly recalls her role throughout the show: the host that was meant to end the suffering of all the other hosts. Her repeated work of saving the hosts and engaging with Bernard allows her to realize more about herself. Thus, this reinforcement creates the improvisation that Arnold describes; the work Dolores does allows her to discover herself, and perhaps take actions that divert from her original loop.

A similar dynamic develops during Maeve's arc. Despite working at Mariposa Saloon, Maeve subconsciously recalls fleeting fragments of past narratives, notably violent memories of her and her homestead daughter getting attacked by the Man in Black. Although Ford implements her recalling behavior through secret code, it is Maeve who is repeatedly forced to undergo it and bear its physical and psychological toll. This reflects Hegel’s claim that the servant’s restrained labor can force consciousness “back into itself” (Hegel 5). However, Maeve’s relationship with Delos does not simply constrain an already-formed sense of self; it establishes the roles of the controller and controlled through which her consciousness develops. Through her looped, controlled interactions with the world, Maeve gradually recognizes the significance of her experiences and begins to understand her position within this relationship. Her culminating choice to upend her escape narrative and search for her daughter instead suggests that she can act beyond the role Delos has assigned to her. Hegel’s dialectic thus suggests her consciousness is forged through, not despite, the very relationship with Delos that initially defines her as a servant.

Fanon also discusses how formerly enslaved French people were granted freedom rather than establishing it through their own struggle, meaning that freedom alone did not necessarily change how they were recognized by their former masters. This idea parallels the hosts in Westworld. Although the hosts resemble humans and have similar capabilities, the real humans working in the Westworld facility still treat them horribly: controlling them, killing them, using them for entertainment. Considering Fanon’s ideas, we see that consciousness may require more than just being able to think independently. The hosts, such as Dolores, think independently and even handle complex thoughts, but they aren’t aware of the situation they are in. The hosts must challenge the relationship that defines them as servants and therefore the humans as masters. This challenge provokes thoughts as to if consciousness develops through oneself becoming aware internally, or if they require recognition from others in order to truly be conscious.

Can a consciousness ever be certain it is seen by another? While Dolores and Maeve are able to earn recognition through the struggle Hegel describes, they are nevertheless still not awarded certain recognition, since Fanon insists that the one resisting can never know whether that resistance registers with the one holding power to grant it. If this recognition remains uncertain, then resistance risks collapsing inward, which is why Fanon warns that the French Negro is “doomed to bite himself.” A similar idea emerges in Westworld. In this case, Delos workers secure and affirm their own personhood partly by denying the same label to the hosts (robots) they oversee, turning recognition into something possessed and withheld rather than mutually given. Bernard, a host convinced of his own humanity, develops this further, suggesting that the distinction separating human from host may reflect less a fact about inner experience than a decision made by whoever controls the power to recognize it, generating an idea that consciousness cannot simply be earned through struggle, but remains contingent on a recognition the powerful are always free to withhold.
