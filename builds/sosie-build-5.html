Build 5

Desire and Deliberation in Hegel and Milton

Both Hegel and John Milton show how deliberation shapes self-consciousness through desire. Deliberation is the contemplation of different possible outcomes and their causal chains before acting in response to a desire; we would not make decisions if we had no wants. In Hegel, the bondsman has to suppress his desires while in a constrained environment under the master’s control. In Milton, Eve must decide whether to eat the forbidden fruit despite God’s warning. Both characters face a conflict between what they want and what limits them from getting what they want. And so, desire creates a reason to consider different possibilities. Yet the two authors show that deliberation does not always look the same.

The main difference between the characters is how they respond to their desires. For Hegel, learning to restrain desire helps the bondsman develop the independence needed to act beyond his immediate wants. The bondsman’s work is described as “desire held in check” (195). Instead of immediately satisfying his desires, the bondsman must hold them back and work to satisfy the master. Through this process, the bondsman begins to “see in the independent being its own independence” (195). In other words, his work allows him to recognize his own abilities rather than remain entirely dependent on the master. The master, on the other hand, satisfies his desires by consuming what the bondsman produces and never gains the same independence. Although the bondsman’s restraint is initially forced, his work helps him develop the independence needed to consider how to act rather than simply react to what he wants.

Milton presents a different relationship between deliberation and desire. Eve similarly faces a choice, but instead of resisting her desire, she uses reasoning to support it. Before eating the fruit, Eve questions why she should fear something when she doesn’t even understand good and evil (773-774). She then asks, “what hinders then / To reach and feed at once both body and mind?” (778-779). Eve knows that God has forbidden the fruit, but she begins to question whether there is a good reason to obey. She considers the possibility that eating it will give her knowledge rather than cause harm. Unlike the bondsman, who learns to resist his desires, Eve convinces herself that satisfying her desire now is the right choice. This does not mean that Eve fails to deliberate; she still considers possible outcomes before acting. The difference is that she is more impulsive and does not resist her desire. Her desire influences which outcome she chooses to believe will happen and benefit her.

However, Eve's decision is not entirely independent. Hegel argues that self-consciousness develops through recognition from another self-consciousness. Milton presents a similar relationship between Eve and Adam. Eve describes Adam as her “guide and head” (442-443), suggesting that she understands herself partly through his authority. Like the bondsman, Eve makes choices within a relationship where someone else has power over her. Yet Adam also faces a choice influenced by desire. After learning that Eve has eaten the fruit, he decides to join her, saying, "If death / Consort with thee, death is to me as life" (953-954). Adam considers the possibility of losing Eve and chooses to share her fate instead. His decision shows that deliberation is not independent of our relationships or desires, since his desire to remain with Eve outweighs the consequences he knows he may face.

Yet, beneath these differences lies a common insight: in both Hegel and Milton, deliberation is driven by desire. I originally understood deliberation as allowing us to carefully consider our actions in order to have greater control over their outcomes. Hegel supports this idea because, although the bondsman is initially forced to restrain his desires, he gradually develops a sense of independence through work and the capacity to act beyond his immediate wants. Milton, however, complicates this claim. Eve is not physically forced to eat the fruit by her desire for knowledge; she considers possible outcomes, but her desire influences her reasoning and leads her to make a choice whose consequences she cannot control. Without desire, neither character would have a reason to consider different possibilities. Desire gives us a reason to consider different futures, and deliberation allows us to consider how to respond. In both readings, consciousness does more than automatically react to circumstances. By imagining different possible outcomes, consciousness can influence which action becomes reality rather than simply following an immediate desire. Deliberation therefore does not guarantee that a rational agent will make the best decision or control an outcome. Rather, it gives us the ability to consider different possibilities before deciding how to act on our desires.
