Zoe Horwitz · Honors 241

Build 1

Recognition and Independence in Fanon

Zoe Horwitz Honors 241, Build 1 Passage:

“For the French Negro the situation is unbearable. Unable ever to be sure whether the white man considers him consciousness in-itself-for-itself, he must forever absorb himself in uncovering resistance, opposition, challenge. [...] The self takes its place by opposing itself, Fichte said. Yes and no. I said in my introduction that man is a yes. I will never stop reiterating that. Yes to life. Yes to love. Yes to generosity. But man is also a no. No to scorn of man. No to degradation of man. No to exploitation of man. No to the butchery of what is most human in man: freedom. Man’s behavior is not only reactional. And there is always resentment in a reaction. Nietzsche had already pointed that out in The Will to Power. To educate man to be actional, preserving in all his relations his respect for the basic values that constitute a human world, is the prime task of him who, having taken thought, prepares to act” (Fanon 10). Independence seems as though it should require no one but oneself. If I understand myself as an independent human being, another person's refusal to recognize me as one should not be capable of taking that independence away. Yet, Fanon's description of the French Black man complicates this assumption. Unable to know whether the white man recognizes him as a “consciousness in-itself-for-itself,” he searches for “resistance, opposition, challenge” (Fanon 10). Fanon therefore presents a strange relationship between independence and recognition: establishing oneself as an independent consciousness seems to require another consciousness to resist.

The uncertainty of this relationship appears in Fanon's description of the French Black man as “unable ever to be sure” (Fanon 10). His uncertainty is not about whether he considers himself human, but whether the white man considers him a consciousness. This distinction makes the white man's perception surprisingly important. If consciousness were entirely internal, another person’s judgment should not create an “unbearable” situation. Fanon instead suggests a difference between knowing oneself to be human and establishing oneself as an independent consciousness in relation to others. Yet what Fanon’s former slave seeks is strange because he does not simply seek another person’s recognition or acceptance; he seeks an opposing consciousness against which he can establish himself. Independence, in that case, may require not approval from another person but the opportunity to exercise one’s agency against another consciousness.

Fanon emphasizes this need when he says that the French Black man must “forever absorb himself” in finding “resistance, opposition, challenge” (Fanon 10). The word “forever” makes establishing oneself as an independent consciousness appear less like something that can finally be achieved and more like a continuous process. This helps explain Fanon's earlier claim that “the former slave needs a challenge to his humanity” (Fanon 10). A challenge to one's humanity initially seems like something a person would want to escape. For Fanon, however, the absence of a challenge may deny the former slave the opportunity to actively establish the humanity that has supposedly been granted to him. The question therefore shifts from whether another person recognizes his humanity to what the former slave himself is able to do in response to another consciousness.

Fanon's comparison between France and the United States helps explain why this challenge might be necessary. Fanon contrasts the French situation with the struggle occurring in the United States, where “the Negro battles and is battled” and “nothing is going to be given for free” (Fanon 10). His emphasis on conflict suggests that freedom received from someone else may not produce the same consciousness as freedom actively pursued. If another person simply declares me free, I remain the object of that person's decision. Opposition, however, gives me the possibility of acting for myself. Perhaps Fanon's former slave seeks resistance because resistance allows him to experience himself not merely as someone upon whom others act, but as someone capable of acting. Independence may therefore depend not on being free from outside forces, but on becoming capable of acting within them.

This possibility becomes more significant when Fanon later insists that “Man's behavior is not only reactional” (Fanon 10). If resistance simply produced an automatic response, then the former slave's actions would still be determined by the forces acting upon him. Fanon seems to distinguish such reaction from action: a person must somehow be capable of encountering a challenge and acting rather than merely reacting. If consciousness is what makes this transition possible, it would have to do more than simply register what is happening. It would have to allow a person to transform an external challenge into an action of his own. Yet this makes Fanon's account of consciousness difficult to resolve. If the French Black man's consciousness has already been shaped by his relationship with the white man, even his search for opposition originates in circumstances outside himself. His resistance could therefore appear to be another reaction to those circumstances rather than a completely independent action. Fanon’s distinction seems to require some capacity to move beyond these external influences, but the source of that capacity remains uncertain. Consciousness is therefore left in a paradoxical position: it is shaped by the forces acting upon it, but must somehow allow a person to produce an action of his own.
