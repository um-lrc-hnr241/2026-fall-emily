Build 1

Fanon and the Problem of Recognition

Emma Goldman

Hoffmann/Angeli

HONORS 241

7 September 2026

Draft of Build 1

Selected passage: “For the French Negro the situation is unbearable. Unable ever to be sure

whether the white man considers him consciousness in-itself-for-itself, he must forever uncover

himself in uncovering resistance, opposition, challenge” (10).

Consider someone knocking on a door. The action alone cannot tell them whether anyone

will answer. They can knock again, louder and more insistently, and still learn nothing until

someone chooses to open it. Frantz Fanon’s description of the French Negro presents a similar

situation: someone is present and straining to be heard on the other side of a door—the white

man’s recognition of the Negro’s self-consciousness—that may never open. The person knocking

possesses consciousness of himself, but cannot determine through his own action whether that

consciousness is recognized by another. Does self-consciousness do anything on its own, or is its

existence contingent on recognition by another consciousness?

Fanon phrases this uncertainty by asking whether the white man considers the French

Negro “consciousness in-itself-for-itself” (10). As Hegel describes, “Self-consciousness exists in

and for itself by way of its existing so for another; that is, it exists only by being recognized” (2).

For Hegel, this recognition is exemplified by the master and slave. Fanon complicates this

relationship by presenting a subject who cannot be sure whether the other recognizes him as a

consciousness at all. A formerly enslaved person may enter a changed social position without

escaping the perceptions that once defined him. Fanon tests Hegel’s model of consciousness,

questioning whether a relationship with such asymmetry can produce the mutual recognition

Hegel's master-slave dialectic requires, or whether it only mimics the form of that relationship

while withholding its substance.

Fanon furthers this uncertainty: “The white man is a master who has allowed his slaves to

eat at his table” (9). While the table creates physical proximity, the white man's allowance

ensures that this proximity is never a claim the slave makes for himself — his presence at the

table remains dependent on the master’s permission. This helps explain Fanon’s claim that the

formerly enslaved person “went from one way of life to another, but not from one life to

another” (9). Despite changes in external circumstances, the relationship through which he

understands himself does not necessarily change with them. If Hegel’s master-slave relation

creates shared ground through recognition, does Fanon’s relationship provide any equivalent

ground, or does the white man’s power to recognize prevent that reciprocity from ever forming?

The master's control extends beyond the physical into the conceptual. Fanon writes that

the French Negro fought for “Liberty and Justice,” but that “these were always white liberty and

white justice; that is, values secreted by his masters” (9). The door metaphor changes here: the

person knocking has not only failed to receive an answer, but has learned how to understand the

meaning of the door from the person on the other side. Can a consciousness become independent

if the very terms through which it understands itself have been formed by another?

Yet Fanon does not portray the French Negro as passively waiting for recognition. He

writes that he “must forever uncover himself” through “resistance, opposition, challenge” (10).

Consciousness therefore appears as something active: the subject continually asserts himself

rather than simply waiting to be acknowledged. This activity sits uneasily beside Fanon's earlier

claim that the subject remains trapped by inherited, "white" values. But Fanon also describes this

activity as potentially self-consuming: “The former slave needs a challenge to his humanity, he

wants a conflict, a riot. But it is too late: The French Negro is doomed to bite himself” (10). The

knocking does not simply go unanswered; unable to elicit a response, it turns inward.The subject

can act, but cannot compel the other consciousness to respond; his resistance, denied an external

target, becomes indistinguishable from a form of psychological self-harm.

In essence, Fanon leaves the efficacy of self-consciousness unresolved. If consciousness

is something a person possesses, then the French Negro’s continual resistance might itself

constitute an achievement, regardless of whether it receives recognition. But if, as Hegel

suggests, self-consciousness exists only through being recognized, then the French Negro’s

activity can never fully complete itself alone. Fanon's insistence on resistance suggests that

self-consciousness is not simply nothing without recognition—but its activity remains

permanently incomplete, forced to assert itself precisely because it can never be sure the door

will open. The question, then, is not simply whether the door will open, but whether the person

knocking can ever become certain of who he is without hearing an answer.

Can a consciousness ever be certain it is seen by another? In the aforementioned passage, Fanon

complicates Hegel's model of mutual recognition by describing the French Negro as a

consciousness that must "forever uncover himself" through "resistance, opposition, challenge,"

yet can never confirm that this resistance, or even consciousness in and of itself, is recognized by

the white man who holds the power to grant or withhold that recognition. If this recognition is

never guaranteed, then resistance risks collapsing inward, which is why Fanon warns that the

French Negro is "doomed to bite himself." A similar idea is portrayed in Westworld: Delos

workers secure and affirm their own personhood partly by denying the same label to the hosts

they oversee, leading to recognition becoming something held and withheld rather than mutually

given. Bernard, a host convinced of his own humanity, develops this further, suggesting that the

distinction separating human from host may be less a fact about inner experience than a decision

made by whoever controls the power to recognize it.
