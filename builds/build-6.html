Build 6

Our Collective Takeaways on Consciousness

Our team has used Hegel to discover the dialectical concept of self-consciousness through engaging with otherness. Hegel argues that humans want to be seen as independent selves while depending on others' recognition to establish that sense of self. Hence, two consciousnesses engage in the master-servant dialectic, and eventually the servant consciousness will usurp the master, like Dolores killing Ford. This is because the servant consciousness fights for recognition after developing itself through desires and work. In contrast, the master consciousness only depends on being seen as superior. Phenomenality exists in the self’s dependence on otherness because no two consciousnesses experience the struggle for recognition the same way, which is why there is a struggle in the first place. Comparing Hegel and Scarry, we recognize that humans cannot project without reciprocation from other consciousnesses. This is why struggle occurs, and the self develops in relation to others' recognition.

How, then, do these relations shape consciousness beyond its initial struggle for recognition? Hegel's master-servant relationship suggests that self-consciousness does not exist prior to its relationship with another, but emerges through interrelated experiences of recognition, fear, and labor. Through narration, we can understand how these moments gain significance through their relation to one another as the servant eventually recognizes his own independence in the object he has formed through labor initially performed in dependence. Fanon complicates this development, though, by illustrating how externally granted emancipation can change a person’s circumstances without transforming the hierarchical relationships through which they understand themselves. Together, these accounts suggest that narration allows us to examine how consciousness develops through relationships, and whether changes in circumstances amount to meaningful transformations of the self. Yet, the perspective through which we interpret that transformation is itself shaped by the relationships we reflect upon.

Can a self be rebuilt by what it never chose? We found that reconsolidation, the way a consciousness revises the link between who it was and who it is now, depends on having something to revise. Hegel’s servant, whose fear is imposed but whose labor is his own in what it makes, reconsolidates, while the master, certain of himself, cannot. Certainty leaves nothing to revise. Fanon complicates this: his Black man receives a freedom he has not fought for, so his new self is shaped for him rather than by him. Yet Hegel’s servant is not fully free either, since the master directs his labor. Reconsolidation therefore means influence rather than exemption: the self is built partly from what it never chose, but it becomes ours through how it revises that material.

If the master still shapes the servant’s labor, can a freedom someone else gave you ever become your own? We think it can, through reconsolidation, the way a consciousness changes the link between who it was and who it is now. Hegel’s servant rebuilds that link through work, which gives him something permanent. Fanon picks a past through action, and because nothing physical holds it, he keeps choosing it. In Paradise Lost, Satan and Adam both lose their past, but Satan yearns for his past self while Adam accepts it and moves on. Adam suggests that a freedom someone else gave you could become your own once you stop looking back for it. But you only stop looking back once something forces you to see that the old self is gone.

What makes us question who we are? Through Hegel, Fanon, and Descartes, we find that reflexivity develops when something disrupts our understanding of ourselves. Hegel’s servant experiences fear but comes to recognize his agency through labor. Fanon shows that being granted freedom does not necessarily produce the same self-understanding as actively claiming it. Descartes complicates this argument because he chooses to question his beliefs rather than being forced into confrontation. These differences matter because disruption alone cannot explain reflexivity. If consciousness develops through questioning and action, perhaps what matters most is not the disruption itself, but how we respond.

The way we respond to disruption depends in part on our ability to deliberate. Deliberation involves reasoning through multiple possibilities over time rather than automatically responding to our circumstances. Hegel’s account of the internalized master-servant struggle suggests that consciousness is not simply determined by external forces, but can reason through conflicting perspectives over time and consider different possibilities for who we could become. Milton illustrates deliberation through Eve, whose desire for the fruit influences her reasoning but does not automatically cause her to act. Instead, she reasons through different possible consequences, such as death or knowledge, before deciding how to act on her desires. Together, Hegel and Milton suggest that consciousness allows us to contemplate different possibilities arising from the same circumstances rather than automatically responding. Ultimately, self-consciousness doesn’t emerge in isolation, but through navigating relationships with others, responding to disruption, and exercising agency under the constraints of our circumstances.
