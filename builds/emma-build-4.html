Reconsolidation and the Servant's Agency

Emma Goldman

Hoffmann/Angeli

HONORS 241

16 October 2026

Build 4

In Build 3, I interpreted Hegel’s master-slave dialectic as a process of reconsolidation in

which consciousness reconstructs its identity through struggle. I also questioned whether this

reconstruction requires an individual to actively participate in redefining themselves. One might

object that reconsolidation does not fully fit Hegel’s conception of consciousness because it

suggests that consciousness reorganizes its own understanding of itself, whereas Hegel presents

consciousness as developing through a process that it does not necessarily control. The slave

does not choose the fear that destabilizes his previous understanding of himself, yet this fear

becomes central to his development: “ …everything solid and stable has been shaken to its

foundations. Yet, this pure universal movement …proves to be the simple, essential nature of

consciousness itself” (5). If consciousness develops through experiences that transform it before

it can understand them, then reconsolidation may incorrectly suggest that consciousness directs a

process that it actually undergoes. And because, for Hegel, self-consciousness comes to be

through mutual recognition, reconsolidation, as a change within one mind, may reduce a

relational struggle to a singular mental adjustment.

This objection challenges my initial understanding of reconsolidation by suggesting that

consciousness may not need to actively direct its own reconstruction. The slave’s transformation

begins with circumstances imposed upon him, rather than an independent decision to redefine

himself. His fear disrupts his previous sense of continuity, thereby forcing him to confront the

instability of his understanding of himself. Reconsolidation may therefore occur not because

consciousness deliberately chooses to reconstruct itself, but because its existing understanding

can no longer accommodate its experiences. From this perspective, the reconstruction of

consciousness begins with a disruption that the individual does not initiate or control.

However, this objection may overlook the significance of the slave’s activity within the

circumstances that transform him. Although the slave does not choose his subordination, he

plays an active role in the labor he performs. Hegel writes, “consciousness, as a worker, comes to

see in the independent being [of the object] its own independence” (5). Through formative labor,

the slave externalizes his fear by fashioning an object and recognizes his own being-for-self

embodied in the world. His consciousness is initially shaped by fear, but it develops through the

activity by which he transforms the world around him. This suggests that reconsolidation does

not require consciousness to initiate its own transformation. Instead, consciousness may

reconstruct its understanding of itself through activities that allow it to recognize its own agency

within circumstances it did not choose.

While Hegel roots self-realization in the worker’s material labor, Frantz Fanon exposes

the limits of this model within the non-reciprocal framework of colonial emancipation. Fanon

questions whether externally imposed change necessarily provides the same opportunity for

self-recognition. He writes that “The upheaval reached the Negroes from without. The Black

man was acted upon” (9). For Fanon, emancipation granted from above bypasses the mutual

struggle for recognition, so the Black man receives his freedom without having won it through

that struggle. If reconsolidation can occur without an individual initiating change, then the

external origins of liberation cannot alone prevent the reconstruction of consciousness. The

Black man may still develop a new understanding of himself as his circumstances change. Yet

Fanon’s account raises the question of whether a change in circumstances allows an individual to

recognize themselves through their own activity, as Hegel’s slave does through labor. The

difference may instead lie not in whether change originates within or outside the individual, but

in whether the individual can participate in the process through which that change acquires

meaning for their sense of self.

Ultimately, this objection has led me to reconsider what reconsolidation contributes to

Hegel’s theory of consciousness. Rather than requiring an individual to consciously direct their

own reconstruction, reconsolidation may describe how consciousness develops a new

understanding of itself through experiences that initially exceed its control. The slave does not

choose the circumstances that destabilize his identity, but through formative labor he confronts

his own agency. Nonetheless, Fanon complicates whether this relationship between activity and

self-recognition can be assumed when liberation is imposed from without. Reconsolidation may

therefore involve more than a change in one’s sense of continuity, demonstrating how

consciousness comes to recognize itself through the process of responding to and transforming

the circumstances that shape it.
