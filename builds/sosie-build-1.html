Build 1

Freedom, Struggle, and Epiphenomenalism

Passage: “Historically, the Negro steeped in the inessentiality of servitude was set free by his master. He did not fight for his freedom…. The upheaval reached the Negroes from without. The black man was acted upon."

Epiphenominalism, the philosophical theory that questions whether consciousness actually does anything, poses a big question in contemporary philosophy of mind. Some philosophers worry that consciousness might be epiphenomenal, meaning that it essentially rides along on top of our physical behavior without causing anything. On this view, two systems could behave in exactly the same way, but only one might have real inner experience, with no way to tell the difference from the outside. In Black Skin, White Masks, Frantz Fanon stages something close to a real-world test of this debate, using Hegel’s ‘master-slave’ story as an interpretive framework. The excerpt worth focusing on is Fanon’s description of how French slaves were freed: "Historically, the Negro steeped in the inessentiality of servitude was set free by his master. He did not fight for his freedom" (8).

Hegel’s theory complicates the idea that consciousness is merely a passive side-effect that performs no causal work of its own. He writes that "it is solely by staking one's life that freedom is won." In other words, becoming truly self-aware does not just happen to a person by simply looking inward; for Hegel, self-awareness develops through risk, struggle, and recognition. It is connected to a person’s actions and relationships rather than existing independently of them.

Fanon takes this idea and connects it to a real-world scenario with French slaves, some of whom are legally freed, and some of whom fought for their freedom. If Hegel is right that self-consciousness comes from struggle, then two situations that look the same on the outside should not actually feel the same on the inside. Whether someone is legally freed or has fought for their freedom, they are still technically free, yet they should not experience their freedom in the same way because only one has undergone the struggle through which Hegel suggests self-consciousness develops. The French slaves were freed without fighting for it: “The upheaval reached the Negroes from without. The black man was acted upon" (9). From an outside perspective, sure, this looks like freedom, but Fanon points out that when freedom was announced, it caused "psychoses and sudden deaths" (9). Together, this supports the idea that the inner experience was completely different since something unseen was causing the freed slaves so much suffering despite them being technically free. Fanon then contrasts this with the American case, where "the Negro battles and is battled.” Here, freedom came from real, violent conflict and where people were left with a more solid, real sense of their own, newfound freedom.

This comparison poses a problem for epiphenomenalism. If consciousness affects how someone responds to freedom, then self-consciousness may not be merely a passive byproduct of social and physical processes. Fanon identifies real symptoms of psychological distress, a lingering sense of not truly being free, particularly when the freed slave wishes for conflict with the white man. Here, the freed slave wishes for the white man to insult him just so that he can fight against him to prove to himself that he can defend and assert himself. This wish is senseless if being legally free were enough to make someone truly free and self-aware. But, it makes complete sense if Hegel is right in saying that being truly free and self-conscious only comes from conflict, and that freedom handed to you is “one-sided and unequal.”

By taking Hegel’s ideas and adding historical examples, Fanon raises a more specific question about consciousness, rather than proving whether freedom exists objectively. Fanon seems to assume that a person’s experience of becoming free is dependent on them participating in a struggle that establishes that freedom. If that experience subsequently changes how that person interprets and experiences their freedom, self-consciousness appears to be causal. But, Fanon’s example alone does not prove that consciousness is the cause of those actions. Instead, it highlights the question of whether self-consciousness is produced by the struggle, produces something new in response to the struggle, or is another effect of the same physical and social processes.
