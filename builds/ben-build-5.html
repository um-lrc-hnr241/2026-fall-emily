Build 5

Fanon, Milton, and Rebuilding the Self

Ben Zemel Honors 241 Build 5

In Build 4, I argued that Fanon reconsolidates by choosing a past through action. Reconsolidation is the process of changing the link a consciousness draws between who it was and who it is now, by selecting past experiences, present circumstances, or future projections. For Fanon, that means rejecting the past race assigns him and choosing one built from refusal. In Milton’s Paradise Lost, Satan, Adam, and Eve all have to rebuild their connection to a past shaped by a more powerful being after their fall. Satan even names all three parts of reconsolidation when his conscience “wakes the bitter memory / Of what he was, what is, and what must be / Worse” (Milton 4.24-26). Both answer with action. Michael tells Adam to “add / Deeds to thy knowledge answerable” (Milton 12.581-82), which connects to Fanon’s goal “To educate man to be actional” (Fanon 10).

The two authors differ on what a consciousness owes the power that shaped its past, because the powers are not the same. Milton’s God created Satan and Adam, so they owe him for their whole existence, and that debt can’t ever be paid off. Satan rebelled to escape it, and then he admits he “understood not that a grateful mind / By owing owes not but still pays” (Milton 4.55-56). He knows the only way back is “submission and that word / Disdain forbids me” (Milton 4.81-82). So he builds his future on opposing God and everything he stands for. “Evil, be thou my good” (Milton 4.110). Adam starts in a similar state of despair, calling himself “To Satan only like both crime and doom” (Milton 10.841). The main difference is that he and Eve repent and ask God for forgiveness, which Satan’s disdain will never let him do. They leave Paradise with “Providence their guide” (Milton 12.647). In Milton, the same power Adam disobeyed is the one that guides his reconsolidation.

Why would Fanon refuse the debt that Milton says Adam has to accept? The white master is not a creator. With the 1794 decree, he handed back a freedom he had taken, yet “The black man contented himself with thanking the white man” (Fanon 9). Adam’s debt to God points him somewhere. The freed slave’s debt to the master just keeps him under the master’s control. Fanon refuses it when he says “I do not have the duty to murmur my gratitude to the white man” (Fanon 12). He chooses his past through action instead, feeling solidarity “every time a man has said no to an attempt to subjugate his fellows” (Fanon 11).

Yet, beneath these differences lies a common insight, namely that reconsolidation fails when a consciousness tries to recover a past it cannot have, and works when it accepts that past as finished and builds from what it does next. When Satan imagines repenting, he pictures getting back “my former state” (Milton 4.94). The freed slave “wants the white man to turn on him and shout” (Fanon 10) so he can have the fight his history never gave him. Adam and Eve never get

their Paradise back, but Michael promises “A paradise within thee, happier far” (Milton 12.587). Adam’s repentance counts as action in Fanon’s sense because it sends him out into the real world, while Satan’s imagined one only looks backwards. This develops my Build 4 claim that Fanon’s continuity has to keep being chosen because no object holds it. Milton shows the same thing with a place. Adam’s continuity used to depend on Paradise, and Michael moves it inside him, where it lasts only while he keeps adding deeds. Milton also complicates my claim about choosing. Adam’s direction comes from Providence and still works, so it doesn’t matter who picks the direction. What matters is whether consciousness acts toward a future or keeps reacting to the past it lost.

Fanon and Milton both use reconsolidation in order to show that a freedom handed over becomes a person’s own only through action, and that the continuity it creates has to keep being remade. Yet, how can reconsolidation allow consciousness to intervene in the stream of physical causality and influence its course? In both readings, the same past leads to opposite outcomes. Satan and Adam both fall, but one opposes God and the other repents. The freed slave and Fanon share a history, but only Fanon acts. If the past alone were the cause, these outcomes should match. What’s different is the link each consciousness draws to that past. Fanon even claims solidarity with revolts he never lived through, so his continuity cannot just be his brain replaying a memory.
