Build 4

Deliberation, Struggle, and Recognition

I have claimed that deliberation, the contemplation of several possible outcomes which do not follow automatically as in physical causation, constitutes the quality of consciousness at work in Hegel and Fanon. My evidence has been drawn from Hegel’s line that “it is solely by staking one’s life that freedom is won” (§187), for staking one’s life requires picturing at least two futures: submit and live unfree, or resist and risk death. Fanon sharpens this by contrasting a man who is “acted upon” with one who “battles and is battled” (220-221). The freed slaves, who received liberation by decree, never made this weighed decision, and the announcement “produced psychoses and sudden deaths” (220). I therefore argued that struggle forces reasoning between alternatives, and that this reasoning produces both action and self-consciousness, so that neither one simply causes the other. However, one might object that if Hegel’s struggle is a leap rather than a weighing, and the flinching servant is the one who deliberates, then deliberation describes the loser rather than the free.

The objection begins from Hegel’s reason for the struggle, which lies in the need for recognition. Each self-consciousness “aims at the death of the other” (§187) and must prove its worth, which sounds like an assertion of a person’s identity, not a comparison of outcomes. The servant’s “essential nature is simply to live” (§189), and Hegel describes him as “seized with dread” (§194). If deliberation is weighing options, the servant deliberates and the master does not, yet Hegel credits the risk with winning freedom, not the retreat. Fanon’s “savage struggle” (218) also sounds like collision rather than weighing. Can the one who flinches really be the one who deliberates? The objection is right that to stake one’s life is not a calculation made in advance, and my claim must give this up. It does, however, assume that deliberation must conclude before the act. The options stay open during the struggle, since “each does itself what it demands of the other” (§182), so each must anticipate their opponent’s response. To act, you must hold several outcomes simultaneously in mind: I kill, I am killed, or the other gives in. None of these happens automatically. Fanon points to this openness when he says the struggler accepts death “but also the possibility of the impossible,” a “world of reciprocal recognitions” that doesn’t yet exist (218). Reaching toward a world of recognition which does not yet exist must mean holding a possibility in mind, and that is not a reflex.

The servant provides a second reason to question the objection. Fear alone does not transform him; Hegel calls it only “the beginning of wisdom” (§195). Work transforms the servant by requiring him to restrain desire. My definition of deliberation describes this act: it is not automatic and the servant must imagine what the object will become and choose not to consume it now. The master, by contrast, acts on desire at once, so his enjoyment is “only a fleeting one” (§195). Both men are “putting one’s own life on the line” (§187), so the master also faced open alternatives. But only the servant acquires the capacity to deliberate because the master hands the work to someone else. This interpretation is tentative, for Hegel says little about the master’s inner life, but a master who relies on the servant’s labor has no reason to exercise restraint. So the servant does not deliberate and lose: he begins in fear and learns to deliberate through delay and discipline.

Fanon’s account reads the same way: “battles and is battled” describes options which change with each side’s actions. Each law struck down or enacted alters the subsequent move, so each side must decide how to respond. What looks like two sides trading blows also forms a series of choices. Fanon marks the difference himself: “Man’s behavior is not only reactional,” and he aims to make man “actional” (222). The freed slave never enters this situation, which is why he is only “acted upon.”

My argument therefore changes in two ways. First, deliberation does not function as a calculation made before the struggle; the risk of death reveals it, and restrained desire creates it. Second, deliberation does not occur within a single mind since the possibilities are shared and each side’s reasoning depends on the other’s. This brings me back to self-consciousness: struggle and joint work build deliberation, so that a person becomes aware of themselves as someone who chooses in the same process which teaches them to choose. This explains why a freedom conferred by an announcement cannot produce that awareness.
