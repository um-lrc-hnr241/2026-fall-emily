Build 4

Reflexivity, Labor, and Agency

Zoe Horwitz Honors 241, Build 4

In Build 3, I claimed that Fanon and Hegel present reflexivity as a quality of consciousness that develops through encounters with another consciousness, rather than independent action alone. Both authors examine how selfhood develops under domination and what it means to become free. Hegel establishes this when he writes that self-consciousness “exists only by being recognized” (Hegel 2): a person becomes conscious of himself through the other. On this reading, Fanon’s French Black man, who searches for “resistance, opposition, challenge” (Fanon 10), seeks a relationship that forces consciousness to turn back on itself.

One might object to this that Hegel's description of the servant undermines this claim. Although Hegel insists on recognition, he says it is through work that “the servant becomes conscious of what he truly is,” describing this process as “a rediscovery of himself by himself” (Hegel 5). The servant seems to find himself in what he makes, not in the master’s recognition. If reflexivity can emerge from one’s own activity, the encounter looks less essential, and Fanon’s emphasis on opposition becomes puzzling: why would the French Black man need opposition?

However, if one looks more closely at the servant, I would argue that labor does not replace the encounter but grows out of it. Before the servant finds himself in his work, he experiences the fear of death, which leaves “everything solid and stable [to be] shaken to its foundations” (Hegel 5). Because he labors under the master’s demand, I interpret his work as a continuation of the encounter in another form. Although the master initially determines the conditions of his labor, the servant eventually recognizes something of himself in what he produces. His “rediscovery of himself by himself” therefore occurs inside the relation of dependence. The objection shows that another consciousness is not sufficient for reflexivity, not that it is unnecessary: fear and dependence initiate the process, while labor allows the servant to recognize his own agency within it.

This distinction changes how I understand Fanon’s description of emancipation. The formerly enslaved French Black man “did not fight for his freedom” and “the black man was acted upon” (Fanon 9). In Build 3, I read this as a missing confrontation between consciousnesses. I take Hegel’s account of the servant to suggest that the problem goes further: although the French Black man’s circumstances changed, he was not necessarily able to recognize himself as an active participant in bringing about that change. This suggests that reflexivity requires more than having one’s circumstances changed by someone else. It requires recognizing one’s own capacity to act within them. Does this mean that freedom granted by another person cannot produce reflexivity? I would not go that far, since a person might still develop an understanding of his agency afterward. However, Fanon’s description suggests that liberation alone does not guarantee this recognition. The distinction between being free and understanding oneself as capable of acting freely becomes essential.

Fanon’s distinction between “reactional” and “actional” behavior raises another question about whether confrontation alone can produce this recognition. His description of the search for “resistance, opposition, challenge” (Fanon 10) initially supports the idea that consciousness develops through confrontation. If opposition alone were sufficient, any response to a challenge would produce reflexivity, yet someone can resist without examining his own motivations. In my reading of Hegel, the servant’s fear begins the process of self-discovery, but fear alone is insufficient; his labor allows him to recognize himself in what he produces. Becoming actional, similarly, requires more than responding to opposition; it seems plausible that it depends on the reflexive recognition that one can shape one’s own experience rather than merely react to another person’s actions. But if Hegel’s servant develops this recognition through labor, what allows Fanon’s French Black man to develop it when his freedom has been determined by someone else? Perhaps the challenge is not simply to find opposition, but to transform that opposition into an opportunity to recognize his own agency.

Hegel’s servant reveals a limitation in my original claim that reflexivity develops through encounters with other consciousnesses. Although these encounters can disrupt self- understanding, reflexivity develops when individuals recognize themselves through their own activity. This deepens Fanon’s contrast between being acted upon and becoming actional, suggesting that freedom requires more than liberation from domination. Rather, it involves recognizing oneself as an agent capable of shaping one’s own experience. Reflexivity, then, is not simply awareness of one’s awareness, but understanding oneself as both shaped by others and capable of forming one’s own self-consciousness.
