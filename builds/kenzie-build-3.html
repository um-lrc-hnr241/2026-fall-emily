The Intersection of Phenomenality and Hegel’s The Phenomenology of Spirit

The intersection of phenomenality and Hegel’s The Phenomenology of Spirit

Two consciousnesses “recognize themselves as mutually recognizing each other” (Hegel 184), but what does this mean? We feel seen through understanding shared aspects of each other’s consciousness. Yet, we like to believe that we independently control ourselves. Phenomenality, one of the essential themes of consciousness, describes that a human experience cannot be reduced to empirical information. The pricelessness of the first-person human perspective on the world cannot be defined; it can only be lived. This idea relates well to Hegel’s view of the formation of consciousness: we feed our self-image through what others perceive of us, yet this constant struggle to engage with our self-image through outside feedback, and to negate the othernesses that influence us, is a difficult-to-define, deeply human experience.

It is difficult for a human to understand that their first-person experience of the world does not come from within, but rather from the relations of the third-person perspectives of others. Hegel quotes that “Self-consciousness is essentially the return from otherness” (167). A popular social psychological theory, Social Identity Theory, explains this very concept: self- identity comes from the groups you associate with; no human is capable of creating a concept of self without “reflection out of the world of sense and perception…” (Hegel 167). For example, I am a college student at the University of Michigan, an eldest daughter, an artist; does my identity exist without these relations? Also, self-consciousness has to “become certain of itself as the essential being… in so doing it proceeds to supersede its own self, for this other is itself” (180). This means that achieving true self-consciousness is a struggle against the self we perceive through others and our desire to become self-defined.

This feedback loop creates a new version of us over and over again, which cannot be understood without phenomenality: no human experiences the same life and identifies with the same groups as another. Perhaps the reason we engage in this conflict continuously is the desire to be seen as independent, despite this being impossible. Fanon, who applies Hegel’s master-slave dialectic to race, describes that “the former slave wants to make himself recognized” (Fanon 7). Us as humans wish to be seen and valued, to be agreed as an independent being by others. This is directly contradictory. Can you be independent, when you have something in relation to be independent of?

A child from their parents, an art piece from an artist; it is paradoxical to establish independence when independence exists when another has to see you as independent. This means that perhaps our sense of self is constantly changing as our consciousness “has lost itself, for it finds itself in another being” (Hegel 168), every time we discover who we are. Can you ever exist for yourself without existing for another? When consciousness exists only for another, Hegel describes it as a matter of “thinghood” (189), meaning you are not self- conscious when you just recognize otherness as not you. Thus, the master-slave dialectic engages because the slave consciousness engaging with the master has “a consciousness forced back on itself” (Hegel 193). This means while the master just exists in relation to others without a desire to self-define, the slave will eventually engage in gaining self-consciousness.

The slave “has been fearful.. Its whole being has been seized with dread” (Hegel 194), conveying that the slave has experienced a first-person suffering that the master has not. This creates struggle, and this struggle allows the slave to recognize that he is alive. With struggle, desire can form. Desire fuels a servile consciousness to engage in a conflict against the master consciousness to end his own struggle, to prove his existence. Thus, Hegel describes a life-and-death conflict, or an “unhappy consciousness” between the two consciousnesses that drives the slave to finally see himself in another, and also characterize otherness “as a negative element” (Hegel 168).

It is because of phenomenality that we cannot comprehend completely what it feels like to live in the experience of another. This internalization of the master-slave dialectic can only be achieved if experienced, not understood or fixed. Hegel’s master-slave dialectic and phenomenality are thus deeply intertwined because humans can only perceive the world through a first-person perspective attempting to understand third-person perspectives, while contradictory attempting to diminish the influence of otherness in the creation of oneself. Though unhappy, this conflict and back and forth struggle is essential to the creation of the self, and is something that cannot be understood beyond simply living through it as a human being.
